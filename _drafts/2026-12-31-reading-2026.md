### 1. [What Is Life?](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/What_Is_Life%3F)

![What Is Life? cover](/assets/posts/books-2026/what-is-life.jpg)

After reading [What is Intelligence]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _posts/2024-12-31-reading-2024.md%}#8-what-is-life) two years ago, I decided to read *What Is Life*, the book that inspired the title and some parts of the book on intelligence. *What Is Life* was written by [Erwin Schrödinger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erwin_Schr%C3%B6dinger), a physicist that won the Nobel Prize in 1938, but that might be most famous to the general public for the [Schrödinger's cat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schr%C3%B6dinger%27s_cat) experiment.

In *What Is Life*, Schrödinger tries to explain how life can come to be, and what physics and chemistry are necessary to explain this. The book goes quite deep into these ideas, which to me, as someone that has neither a physics or chemistry background, could occassionally be difficult to follow without looking up many terms.

Since the book was written, apparently some of the ideas in it have fallen out of favor, too, which is also hard to spot as someone that was not too deep into the material before. However, despite this, I found it to be a really intriguing read, with many cool ideas and things to learn about.

### 2. [The Courage to Be Disliked](https://www.amazon.com/Courage-Be-Disliked-Phenomenon-Happiness/dp/1668065967)

![The Courage to Be Disliked cover](/assets/posts/books-2026/courage-to-be-disliked.jpg)

*The Courage to Be Disliked* is an amazing book with a not-so-great title. The title makes it sound like a self help book, while it really is much more a book about philosophy and criticially examining things. However, I also understand why the title was chosen, as it probably attracts a lot more people to the book than some high-level title would have.

The book consists of a discussion between a philosopher and a young man, who wants to learn to be happy. The pilosopher teaches him about [Alfred Adler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Adler)'s philosophy. I quite enjoyed the read, and really liked the Socratic way that the conversation between the skeptical youth and the philosopher unfolds. 

### 3. [Measuring the World](https://www.amazon.com/Measuring-World-Novel-Daniel-Kehlmann/dp/0307277399)

![Measuring the World cover](/assets/posts/books-2026/measuring-the-world.jpeg)

[Carl Friedrich Gauss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Friedrich_Gauss) and [Alexander von Humboldt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_von_Humboldt) are two of the greatest scientists of the 18th and 19th century. Gauss made many significant contributions to mathematics and astronomy, while Humboldt was a polymath and explorer, traveling through many parts of the Americas.

*Measuring the World* tells a historical, fictional story of their two lives, which intertwine eventually. Both their lives are incredibly interesting, in intellectual terms as well as in the sense that they lived through many adventures and saw many interesting places. However, besides this, the book is also just incredibly funny, making me laugh out regularly, and turning the book into an amazing read.

### 4. [Impro: Improvisation and the Theatre](https://www.amazon.com/Impro-Improvisation-Theatre-Keith-Johnstone/dp/0878301178)

![Impro: Improvisation and the Theatre cover](/assets/posts/books-2026/impro.jpg)

In 2026, I tried improvisation for the first time. It was both a scary and a fun experiment at the same time. After the first couple of classes, I decided to look for a book to read more about how improvisation ought to work, and decided on *Impro*.

The book was written by [Keith Johnstone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keith_Johnstone), a pioneer of improvisational theather. It starts by recounting his improvisation career, but then mostly focuses on advice, structured into the four components of status, spontaneity, narration, and masks and trance.

I quite enjoyed the book, but would have wished for there to be more practical exercises and recorded examples of improvisation. As is, it felt more like a book for people that are already fairly deeply into the craft of improvisation.
